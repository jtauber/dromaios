Skip the addition.

Leave the destination at 0108 · Skip the addition. Take one step: the load puts 41 into A, and PC moves from 0100 to 0103.

The next instruction is JMP 0108H: jump to address 0108. Take another step and watch PC. A stays 41, but the next-instruction marker moves directly to the store.

The store writes 41. The jump passed over the addition at 0106, so it never changed A. The program contains four instructions, but this path executes only three.

Follow the skip path ↑

An instruction for PC.

Until now, each instruction left PC pointing just beyond its own bytes. The jump instead puts its destination address into PC. That is where the processor fetches the next instruction.

The jump itself occupies three bytes at 0103, 0104, and 0105. The byte after it is at 0106. But with destination 0108, that is where PC ends up.

JMP changes PC without changing A, the flags, or memory. Its destination is an address, not a number to add to A and not a byte to copy into A.

How is the destination stored?

C3 is the jump’s opcode. The following two bytes hold its destination, low byte first: 08 01 means address 0108. Thus the whole instruction is C3 08 01. The H in JMP 0108H marks a hexadecimal address.

Present, but not executed.

The addition’s bytes, C6 01, are still at 0106 and 0107. The jump does not erase them. They simply are not fetched on this path.

After the jump, the shaded bytes belong to the jump itself. The store has the next-instruction marker. The addition is marked Skipped · not fetched, and stays visible so you can compare the program in memory with the path taken through it.

The PC path comes from the instructions that actually ran: 0100 → 0103 → 0108 → 010B. As before, the lesson stops offering steps at the end, 010B. That boundary is not a halt instruction.

Choose another destination.

Press Start again, then choose 0106 · Run the addition before stepping. The jump’s bytes become C3 06 01, and its label becomes JMP 0106H. You have edited its destination in RAM; nothing has run yet.

The jump goes to 0106, so the addition runs and A becomes 42. The store writes 42. This path takes four steps: 0100 → 0103 → 0106 → 0108 → 010B.

Run the addition ↑

The jump runs in both versions. We choose its destination in advance; the processor does not make a decision about A’s value. This is an unconditional jump.

The destination choice locks after the first step. Start again restores the original data, processor state, and skip path. These are lesson controls; the processor itself follows the instruction bytes.

So far our jumps move forward. A jump back to an earlier instruction can make the processor repeat part of a program. That will be our next step.
